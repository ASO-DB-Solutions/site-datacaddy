# 2026-09-13 — The site stops making claims it cannot back, and its controls start telling the truth

**Source**: nine commits, `b3c83ea` through `1ce5042` (11:27–12:52); pull requests [#13](https://github.com/ASO-DB-Solutions/site-datacaddy/pull/13) through [#16](https://github.com/ASO-DB-Solutions/site-datacaddy/pull/16). Everything described here is live at [datacaddy.co](https://datacaddy.co).

Yesterday ended with three things blocked on other people. Two of them cleared overnight, and the
day was spent on a single theme that connects the rest: the site was saying things it could not
support, and controls were letting people attempt things that could not work.

## Two blockers cleared, and the runbook that was wrong about one of them

`app.datacaddy.co` now exists with a valid certificate, so the **Sign in** link ships normally — it
had been pointing at a bare IP over plain HTTP and was held back from the public build for that
reason. And **Netlify form detection was enabled and the site redeployed**, so the contact form
accepts submissions for the first time since launch.

That second one closes the failure yesterday's entry described. It also corrected the form runbook,
which had still been telling the owner the form was broken after it had been fixed. Two of the three
items that carried a red "not shippable" mark are now gone; the unverified cloud pricing remains.

## The calculator was quoting a price for a product that does not exist

The cost calculator shows a prospect what their database would cost on four clouds. Researching the
Oracle Cloud column found two problems, and only one of them was a number.

**Oracle Cloud has no managed Microsoft SQL Server.** On OCI, SQL Server runs under the customer's
own licence on compute instances they administer — there is no equivalent to Amazon RDS for SQL
Server or Azure SQL. The calculator was quoting a managed monthly price for it anyway. That pairing
now explains what Oracle Cloud actually offers instead, which is a different operating model rather
than a cheaper one. It answers the question the prospect was really asking, which a number never
would have.

**The Oracle prices themselves could not be sourced, and the honest move was to say so rather than
to find a better guess.** Oracle publishes managed-database rates per OCPU and per GB instead of as
named instance types, so there is no comparable figure to quote. Its own price pages refuse
automated requests, and the independent sources carrying figures disagree with each other and
disclaim themselves — one states plainly that its numbers are orientation, not quotation.
Substituting one untraceable figure for another would have repeated the mistake with more
confidence behind it.

So every column now names the service and the region its figures come from, and the Oracle column
additionally says on the page that it is an estimate rather than a quoted list price. A reader can
now check any of the four, which was not previously possible. These are commercial numbers shown to
prospects, and a figure a prospect's own architect can dismantle in a meeting costs more than a
missing one. The reasoning is recorded as [ADR-0006](../adr/0006-a-price-the-site-cannot-source-is-labelled-not-quoted.md).

Clearing it needs one thing nobody here can do: a few minutes in an OCI account with the Cost
Estimator. Until then the caveat stays visible, which is the intended discomfort.

## The designer's layout, restored

The call-to-action section had been rebuilt at some point into a two-column arrangement that was
not in Nina's design. The consequence was invisible until measured: her layout holds the text at a
constant 640 pixels wide, and the invented one let it swing between 587 and 942 depending on the
viewport, so line lengths changed as the window moved. Reverted to what she drew.

## Controls that cannot act now say so before they are used, not after

Two changes, same idea.

**The contact form's send button stays dimmed** until name, email and company are filled and the
address looks like an address, with a line underneath naming what is still missing — in both
languages. Email checking is deliberately loose: no `@`, no dot, a one-character ending or a space
inside is rejected, and nothing else. Stricter rules reject addresses that genuinely work, and the
only real test of an address is whether mail to it arrives.

**The calculator's destination buttons dim** for clouds that have no managed version of the chosen
engine — picking Oracle dims Azure and Google Cloud, picking SQL Server dims Oracle Cloud. The
calculator already refused those combinations; it simply waited until after the click to say so.

Neither control is switched off in the technical sense, and that was the deliberate part. A truly
disabled button disappears from keyboard navigation, so someone using a keyboard or a screen reader
meets a control they cannot reach and is told nothing about why. Both stay reachable: pressing the
dimmed send button marks every outstanding field and jumps to the first, and clicking a dimmed
cloud opens the explanation of what it offers instead. "Why is that one grey?" is worth answering.

One bug is worth recording because of how it was caught. The destination button was setting its
tooltip twice, and the second quietly overwrote the first. The build stayed green — the build never
checks types — and the separate typecheck step failed. That step exists for exactly this, and this
is the second time it has earned its place.
