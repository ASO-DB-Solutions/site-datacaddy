# 2026-09-11 — datacaddy.co goes live, quietly and on purpose

**Source**: six commits, `d36d026` through `b49624b` (14:02–17:34); pull request
[#1](https://github.com/ASO-DB-Solutions/site-datacaddy/pull/1). The Netlify account and the DNS
records were created by the owner outside the repository, so the moment the site actually went
live leaves no commit of its own — it was verified directly against the live address instead.

**The site is online at [datacaddy.co](https://datacaddy.co)**, with a valid certificate, both
languages answering, and search engines still asked to stay away. That last part is deliberate:
the site is live but not launched, and the two are being kept separate on purpose.

## The domain turned out to constrain the plan

The intention was to move the domain's DNS into Microsoft, alongside the company's other services.
Checking rather than assuming turned up two things that closed that option.

`datacaddy.co` is registered at **Cloudflare**, and Cloudflare requires domains registered with it
to keep using its own name servers. Moving DNS to Microsoft would have meant transferring the
registration to a different registrar first — weeks of work and a fee, before anything could be
configured.

It also turned out to be unnecessary. **Microsoft 365 does not host DNS**; it only specifies which
records must exist, and those work perfectly well where the domain already lives. The requirement
behind the request — a working `info@datacaddy.co` — is satisfied without moving anything.

Worth noting separately: `datacaddy.com` belongs to someone else. The designer's export used
`hello@datacaddy.com` as the site's contact address, which would have pointed customers at a
domain the company does not control.

## What a public repository publishes

The site's code is public, which was a deliberate choice with real benefits. What had never been
written down is what that actually exposes, so the whole surface was audited and documented.

**No secret was found exposed**, and the audit specifically confirmed that the workstation name,
the production server name, the bot's address and every IP address are absent.

What it did find is five things that are public by default rather than by decision — including a
personal email address permanently attached to every commit, which cannot be corrected
retroactively. None is urgent. All are now listed so they can be decided rather than discovered.

Three facts from that review are the kind people reliably get wrong. A "secret" stored in a
website's build settings is written straight into the file every visitor downloads — the hosting
control panel calls it a protected setting, and it is not. Every security certificate ever issued
for a domain is recorded in a public, permanent register, so a private-sounding address like
`staging.datacaddy.co` is discoverable minutes after it exists. And on a public project, the logs
of automated checks are readable by anyone.

Six rules came out of that and were written up as proposals rather than adopted quietly, since
they change how the team works.

## A diagram that hid the thing it was drawn to show

The publishing diagram in the setup guides had a defect the owner spotted: the review step
appeared, an arrow reached it, and nothing left it. Review, the automated checks and the approval
that actually gates production were all invisible in a picture whose job was to show exactly that.

Redrawn in two clearly separated halves — everything that happens to a proposed change, and
production, which is built only from approved work. The stakeholder document gained a diagram of
its own, having had none.

## The Portuguese stopped reading like a translation

A review pass over the Portuguese edition, then a second one at the owner's request. The first
fixed wording: a label describing an automated check used a verb that means physically blocking
something, and several phrases were English grammar wearing Portuguese words.

The second pass was the one that earned its place. It found a link inside both guides that led
nowhere, in both languages — and a **factual** error in the stakeholder document, which said three
DNS records where the guides said two. The table lists three rows, but one is an alternative to
another, not an addition. The document going to the person doing the work was the one carrying the
wrong number.

## Decisions stopped being folklore

Three decision records were written: leaving the Figma tooling, giving each language its own
address, and choosing Netlify. The Netlify record spends most of its length on the options that
lost, because they were close — one of them was the option the company was already set up for, and
it lost on balance rather than on capability.
